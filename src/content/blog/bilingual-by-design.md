## Begin with the experience

An interface can contain accurate translations and still feel like it was designed for someone else. The reading order may be awkward, a label may run into an icon, or a navigation cue may point in an unexpected direction.

A useful starting principle is to design the two language experiences together. That makes the relationship between them a product decision rather than a cleanup task at the end.

## Examine a complete task

Take one flow from entry to completion. Put the English and Arabic versions side by side and inspect what the person needs to understand at each step.

- What establishes the page’s hierarchy?
- Which elements communicate direction?
- What happens when a label becomes longer?
- How are mixed scripts, identifiers and numbers displayed?
- Does an error explain the same next step in both languages?

These questions help reveal the assumptions built into a component. A mirrored layout is a starting point for review, not proof that the experience is complete.

## Make the rules reusable

Once a directionality decision has been reviewed, capture it where the next designer or engineer will find it. Put examples beside the component, including a longer label and an error state.

The most useful guidance explains why an element changes, not just how it looks. That makes the rule easier to apply when the next screen introduces a new combination of content.

## Leave room for language expertise

A working prototype can expose layout problems, but it cannot replace review by people who understand the language and context. Include that review in the delivery process and test the interaction with the people who will use it.
