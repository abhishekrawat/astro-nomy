## Ask what the person needs to know

A desktop display has a different relationship with attention from a phone app. It is already present. A person might look at it for a moment while doing something else.

That makes hierarchy the central design problem. Decide which piece of information earns the first glance, which provides context and which can wait for an interaction.

## Let the setting shape the interface

An interface that looks clear on a laptop can be difficult to read across a desk. The enclosure, angle, lighting and display size are part of the experience.

A useful prototype test begins in the place the object will live. Observe the distance and angle from which it is read, then adjust the design around those conditions.

## Design for missing information

A connected display must handle the absence of fresh data. An old estimate that looks current can be less useful than an honest unavailable state.

Design that state alongside the successful one. Give the person enough context to decide whether to rely on the display or check another source.

## Give the object a narrow job

Small connected devices are an opportunity to practise restraint. Extra information is easy to add; earning attention without asking for more of it is harder.

The question to return to is simple: does this addition make the next glance more useful?
