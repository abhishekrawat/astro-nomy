## At a glance

**The opportunity:** Turn the information already available through a Health Information Exchange into a useful patient experience.

**The design question:** How can people find the information they need without having to understand the structure of a clinical record?

**The lens:** Comprehension, continuity and equal consideration for Arabic and English.

The following decision narratives are an editorial example for this portfolio. They illustrate how the case study can be told; they are not a verified account of research, delivery decisions or measured outcomes. The original project overview below has been retained.

## Project context

This was my first major healthcare project of its scale.
We had built a solid backend HIE and a robust provider facing portal.
The next big challenge was to have a patient facing app that would leverage on the HIE and help patients to:

- View current and historical medical records
- View and book appointments
- View lab and radiology reports
- See analysis of specific trends (eg. glucose levels over a period of time)
- View medications
- View and compare health profile

The population referenced in the project description describes the intended reach of the health ecosystem. It should not be read as verified active usage of this application or as a measured design outcome.

## Decision 01: Start with the patient’s question

A clinical record is organised to document care. A patient may arrive with a different question: “What happened at my last visit?” or “Where is my latest report?”

A useful design direction is to organise the first layer around recognisable tasks, while keeping the original clinical information accessible. A summary should help someone locate a record without silently changing its meaning.

**Alternative to examine:** A comprehensive list of records on the first screen. It makes the available information explicit, but puts more interpretation work on the patient.

**Evidence to add:** An annotated before-and-after screen, the research observation that led to the change, and what participants could do after it.

> The design challenge is to reduce the effort of finding information while preserving its clinical context.

## Decision 02: Give both languages a first-class experience

An Arabic interface deserves its own layout review. Direction, text expansion and the placement of navigation cues all affect how a page is understood.

## Decision 03: Make the next step visible

Finding a report, understanding an appointment and returning to an overview are connected tasks. Each screen needs to communicate where the person is and what they can do next.

A useful case-study comparison would show a complete task rather than isolated final screens: entry point, detail, action and return.

**Tradeoff to explain:** How the team balanced information density against the need to orient someone quickly, especially on a small screen.

## Leading the work

This is the place to distinguish design ownership from team contribution. A strong leadership story should explain how the problem was framed, who influenced the decision, and how disagreements were resolved.

| Leadership question | Evidence to include |

| Who did I bring together? | Product, engineering and clinical partners |

| What did the team carry forward? | A reusable pattern or working agreement |

## Outcomes and learning

The existing screens demonstrate the product direction. They do not establish adoption, usability improvement or business impact on their own.

Before publishing a final version, add an approved outcome with its timeframe and source. If quantitative data is unavailable, use attributable qualitative feedback or a clearly described delivery result.

The most useful closing reflection will name a decision worth repeating and one that would change with better evidence.
